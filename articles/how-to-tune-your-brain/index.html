How to Tune Your Brain?

A reflection on training the mind through faith, perspective at work, and staying connected to the roots of family.

JJ

By Jedidiah Jsk

Aug 2026 • 12 min read

Every mind is constantly being tuned. A conversation can change our mood, disappointment can change the way we see people, success can change how we see ourselves, and faith, work, and family continuously influence the way we think.

We cannot control everything that enters our lives, but we can train the way our minds respond to it. I call this tuning the brain - not ignoring pain, fear, anger, or disappointment, but refusing to let our first thought automatically become the thought that controls our actions.

In roughly thirty years of living, learning, failing, working, believing, and observing people, I have found three areas where this matters to me most: God. Work. Family.

"You cannot control every thought that knocks on the door of your mind, but you can decide which ones you invite to stay."

1. God - Tune the Mind Toward Faith

For me, everything begins here. Putting God first does not mean expecting a life without problems; it means believing that no problem has the final authority over our life. There will be situations where our knowledge reaches its limit, and even after seeking advice, working harder, and preparing better, we may still not know what happens next. That is where faith becomes more than a religious word - it becomes an unseen inner support that brings peace even when the circumstances have not changed.

"In uncertainty, putting God first replaces the need to understand everything with the strength to trust through anything."

When a door closes, faith can teach patience instead of frustration; when a door opens, it can teach gratitude instead of entitlement. Faith matters not only when life is difficult, but also when life is going well.

Failure can lead the mind toward hopelessness, while success can lead it toward pride. Putting God first helps me recognize that I can work hard and celebrate achievement while still being grateful for the people, opportunities, timing, circumstances, and blessings that helped make it possible.

My Christian faith teaches me that God has worked miracles, continues to work in ways we may not immediately recognize, and that what seems impossible to us is not necessarily the end of the story. That belief tunes my mind differently: in hardship, do not lose hope; in uncertainty, do not surrender to fear; in success, do not become proud; and in waiting, do not become impatient. That is the first tuning.

2. Work - Tune the Mind Before You Judge the Situation

Work occupies an enormous part of adult life. For many people, the working day extends far beyond the hours shown on a timesheet - from preparing and travelling to spending hours solving problems, dealing with people, and finally returning home. Because we invest so much of ourselves into work, disappointment there can become deeply personal.

Imagine working for a large global pharmaceutical and medical technology company, giving years of hard work and sometimes more than twelve hours of your day to your career. Then a promotion becomes available, but someone else receives it. People around you immediately offer explanations: He knows the manager personally, There is some family connection or The decision was already made. Perhaps some of these statements are true, and perhaps none of them are. But before knowing the answer, the brain can begin writing an entire story from incomplete information. You feel betrayed, your motivation falls, and you begin viewing everything through the belief that you were treated unfairly.

This is precisely where I believe the mind needs tuning. It does not mean blindly accepting unfair treatment; genuine favoritism, discrimination, or unethical behavior should be recognized and addressed. But before reaching that conclusion, slow the mind down. Instead of accepting the first explanation, create several. Step outside your own position and ask how others might have reacted in your place, what they might have noticed that you missed, and what they might have done differently. The purpose is not to copy someone else, but to challenge your own thinking before making a decision.

"Think 4 × 2ⁿ → 360°: multiply your perspectives until you see the situation from every angle. Your first thought is only one angle - never mistake it for the whole truth."

Only then begin narrowing the possibilities. Why was the other person selected? What experience, responsibilities, or qualities did they demonstrate? What skills might I still need to develop? Could favoritism have played a role? Keep that possibility on the table too, but do not make it the entire table without evidence. Self examination gives some control back to you: perhaps you need stronger communication, greater technical knowledge, more visibility, or more leadership experience or perhaps the decision genuinely was unfair. Either way, separate what happened from the story your mind immediately created about what happened, and make the healthiest next move you can.

"A tuned mind does not deny disappointment; it refuses to let disappointment become the only explanation."

3. Family - Tune the Mind Back Toward the Roots

Recently, while reading, I came across an idea that stayed with me: a family should be sustained through respect, faithfulness, responsibility, and generosity. It immediately connected with something I have understood through the Word of God and through life.

"Love keeps a family together. Respect keeps it grounded. Forgiveness keeps it from falling apart."

We choose our friends, colleagues, and many of the people we allow close to us, but family is different. We arrive in this world and discover that a tree already exists - parents, grandparents, brothers, sisters, uncles, aunts, cousins, generations and stories that began before we did. We simply become another branch. Yet disagreements can separate families for five, ten, fifteen years or even longer. Sometimes separation is necessary where there is abuse, danger, or persistent destructive behavior, but many families separate because pride lasts longer than the argument. One person waits for an apology, the other waits for the first call, and while they wait, years pass, children grow, and parents age.

This is another place where the brain needs tuning. We have to ask whether winning an argument is worth losing a relationship. I think of family as a tree: consider a fruit that falls from its branch before its time. Once separated, it cannot control where it lands stone, water, grass, soft soil, or hard ground and it faces the rain, cold, wind, and heat without receiving nourishment from its roots. The fruit that remains connected to the tree faces the same weather, but it still has something holding and nourishing it.

"The storm does not decide what survives; the roots do. What we remain connected to determines how strongly we stand."

Family can work in a similar way. In difficult seasons, people who know where we came from can sometimes see things we cannot. We may be educated, financially successful, physically strong, technologically knowledgeable, or professionally accomplished, but no person knows everything. What I know comes from the life I have lived; another family member has lived a different life and may understand people, risks, traditions, skills, or parts of the world that I do not. This does not mean every family member is always right. It means wisdom can exist outside our own perspective, and sometimes the person we think knows less than us simply knows something different from us.

The Brain Is Tuned Through Repetition

None of these changes happen because we read one article or make one decision. A musical instrument does not remain perfectly tuned forever, and neither does the mind. Life continuously pulls it out of alignment - fear toward panic, success toward pride, disappointment toward bitterness, and isolation toward believing that we need nobody.

So we tune it again, and again, and again. For me, the process has gradually become simple: when life becomes uncertain, return to God; when work becomes disappointing, return to perspective; and when relationships become difficult, return to the roots before cutting the branch.

The situation may remain exactly the same, but the mind facing that situation will not.

Conclusion

Perhaps maturity is not reaching a point where difficult thoughts stop appearing, but learning what to do when they arrive. I cannot control every closed door, every decision another person makes about me, every disagreement within a family, or everything God allows to happen. But I can continue working on the mind that meets those situations.

Faith can keep hardship from turning into hopelessness. Perspective can keep disappointment from turning into bitterness. Family can remind us that independence does not require isolation. After roughly thirty years of life, I certainly do not claim to understand everything; if anything, growing older has shown me how much remains unknown.

But one lesson continues becoming clearer: a peaceful life is not created only by arranging the world around us. Sometimes it begins by tuning the world within us.
