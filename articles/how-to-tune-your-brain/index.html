How to Tune Your Brain?

A reflection on training the mind through faith, perspective at work, and staying connected to the roots of family.

JJ

By Jedidiah Jsk

Aug 2026 • 12 min read

Every mind is constantly being tuned.

A conversation can change our mood. A disappointment can change the way we see people. Success can change how we see ourselves. Family shapes our values. Work tests our patience. Faith determines what we hold on to when many things in life do not make sense.

We cannot control everything that enters our lives, but I have slowly come to believe that we can train the way our minds respond to what enters them.

I call this *tuning the brain*.

I am not speaking about neuroscience, nor am I claiming that every difficult emotion can simply be thought away. Pain is real. Disappointment is real. Anger, uncertainty, betrayal, and fear are real.

Tuning the brain means something different. It means refusing to allow the first thought that enters our mind to automatically become the thought that controls our actions.

In roughly thirty years of living, learning, failing, working, believing, observing people, and being part of a family, I have found three areas where this matters to me most:

God. Work. Family.

*"You cannot control every thought that knocks on the door of your mind, but you can decide which ones you invite to stay."*

1. God - Tune the Mind Toward Faith

For me, everything begins here.

Putting God first does not mean expecting a life without problems. It means believing that no problem has the final authority over our life.

There will be situations where our knowledge reaches its limit. We can calculate possibilities, ask people for advice, work harder, prepare better, and still reach a point where we simply do not know what happens next.

That is where faith becomes more than a religious word.

In difficult seasons, faith becomes an unseen inner support. The circumstances may not immediately change, but something within us can. Fear does not disappear completely, but it no longer has to occupy the entire mind.

There is peace in believing that I do not have to understand everything in order to continue moving forward.

*"In uncertainty, putting God first replaces the need to understand everything with the strength to trust through anything."*

When a door closes, faith can teach patience instead of frustration. When a door opens, faith can teach gratitude instead of entitlement. And I believe this becomes equally important when life is going well.

Failure can damage the mind through hopelessness, but success can damage it through pride. When we begin believing that every achievement happened only because of our intelligence, strength, discipline, or ability, success can quietly convince us that we are the sole authors of our lives.

Putting God first changes that perspective.

I can work hard and still be grateful. I can celebrate an achievement without believing I am greater than the people around me. I can recognize my effort while also recognizing the opportunities, people, timing, circumstances, and blessings that allowed that effort to produce something.

My Christian faith teaches me that God has worked miracles, that He continues to work in ways we may not immediately recognize, and that what seems impossible to us is not necessarily the end of the story.

That belief tunes my mind differently.

In hardship, it tells me not to lose hope. In uncertainty, not to surrender to fear. In success, not to become proud. In waiting, not to become impatient.

That is the first tuning.

2. Work - Tune the Mind Before You Judge the Situation

Work occupies an enormous part of adult life.

For many people, the working day is much larger than the hours shown on a timesheet. It begins when we wake up, prepare, travel, enter the workplace, spend hours solving problems and dealing with people, and finally return home.

Because we invest so much of ourselves into work, disappointment there can become deeply personal.

Imagine that you work for a large global pharmaceutical and medical technology company. For years, you have worked extremely hard. Some days, your work consumes twelve hours between leaving home and returning. You take responsibility seriously. You believe you have earned the next opportunity.

Then a promotion becomes available, but you do not receive it. Another employee does. Almost immediately, people around you begin explaining why.

"He knows the manager personally."

"There is some family connection."

"The decision was already made."

"They never intended to promote you."

Perhaps some of those statements are true. Perhaps none of them are.

But something dangerous can happen before you know the answer: your brain begins writing the entire story from incomplete information.

You feel betrayed. Your motivation falls. You stop putting in the same effort. You begin interpreting every interaction through the belief that you were treated unfairly.

This is precisely where I believe the mind needs tuning.

Not because you should blindly accept unfair treatment. If genuine favoritism, discrimination, or unethical behavior exists, it should be recognized and addressed appropriately. But before reaching that conclusion, ask yourself whether you have examined the situation or merely reacted to it.

Slow the mind down.

Instead of accepting the first explanation, create several.

Before deciding what the situation means, step outside your own position. Ask how others might have reacted if they were in your place, what they might have noticed that you missed, and what they might have done differently. The goal is not to copy someone else's decision, but to challenge your own thinking before arriving at one.

*"Think 4 × 2ⁿ → 360°: start with four possibilities, keep multiplying your perspectives, and do not stop at the first explanation. Expand your thinking until you can see the situation from every possible angle. Your first thought is only one angle—never mistake one angle for the whole truth."*

Only after looking at the situation from those different angles should we begin narrowing the possibilities and making the best call we can.

Why might the manager have selected the other person? What experience did that person have? What responsibilities have they already demonstrated? What skills does the next position require? What qualities might my manager see in them that I have not yet developed?

What feedback have I previously received? What could I improve before the next opportunity?

And yes, could favoritism have played a role? Keep that possibility on the table too. Just do not make it the entire table without evidence.

*"A tuned mind does not deny disappointment; it refuses to let disappointment become the only explanation."*

This shift is powerful because blaming another person gives them control over your next move. Self-examination gives some of that control back to you.

If I immediately decide that my manager is the problem, I may leave the situation angry but unchanged. If I examine myself honestly, I might leave disappointed but stronger.

Maybe I discover that I need better communication skills. Maybe I need greater technical knowledge. Maybe I need to demonstrate leadership before receiving the title. Maybe I need more visibility. Maybe the decision genuinely was unfair.

Even then, I can decide what the healthiest next move is instead of allowing anger to decide it for me.

This does not mean becoming emotionless. It means separating what happened from the story my mind immediately created about what happened.

That distinction can change a career.

3. Family - Tune the Mind Back Toward the Roots

Recently, while reading, I came across an idea that stayed with me:

A family should be sustained through respect, faithfulness, responsibility, and generosity.

It immediately connected with something I have understood through the Word of God and through life:

*"Love keeps a family together. Respect keeps it grounded. Forgiveness keeps it from falling apart."*

Family relationships are complicated because they are relationships we usually did not choose.

We choose friends. We choose colleagues. We choose many of the people we allow close to us. But family?

We arrive in this world and discover that a tree already exists.

Parents. Grandparents. Brothers. Sisters. Uncles. Aunts. Cousins. Generations and stories that began before we did. We simply become another branch.

Yet today, disagreements can separate families for five, ten, fifteen years or even longer.

Sometimes separation is necessary, particularly where there is abuse, danger, or persistent destructive behavior. Staying connected should never mean accepting harm without boundaries.

But many families do not separate because safety required it. They separate because pride lasted longer than the argument.

One person waits for an apology. The other waits for the first person to call. Years pass. Children grow. Parents age. People change.

And eventually everyone remembers the argument but forgets why reconciliation once seemed impossible.

This is another place where the brain needs tuning.

We have to ask ourselves whether winning an argument is worth losing a relationship.

I think of family as a tree.

Consider a fruit that falls from its branch before its time. Once separated from the tree, it has no control over where it lands. It may hit stone, water, grass, soft soil, or hard ground. Rain reaches it. Heat reaches it. Cold reaches it. Wind reaches it.

Most importantly, it no longer receives nourishment from its roots.

Now consider the fruit still connected to the tree. The same rain falls. The same wind blows. The same sun produces heat.

The difference is not the weather.

*"The difference is what the fruit remains connected to while the weather changes."*

Family can work in a similar way.

When difficult seasons arrive, people who know where we came from can sometimes see things we cannot.

We may believe that because we are educated, financially successful, physically strong, technologically knowledgeable, or professionally accomplished, we already know what is best.

But knowledge does not work that way.

The world is too large. No person knows everything.

What I know comes partly from the places I have been, the things I have studied, and the experiences I have survived. Another member of my family has lived a different life.

They may understand something about people that I do not. They may recognize a danger I have never encountered. They may know a tradition, skill, history, profession, community, or part of life that my education never taught me.

Connecting those experiences can lead to wiser decisions.

This does not mean every family member is always right. It means wisdom sometimes exists outside our own perspective.

And sometimes the person we think knows less than us simply knows something different from us.

The Brain Is Tuned Through Repetition

None of these changes happen because we read one article or make one decision.

A musical instrument does not remain perfectly tuned forever. Neither does the mind.

Life continuously pulls it out of alignment.

Fear can tune it toward panic. Success can tune it toward pride. Disappointment can tune it toward bitterness. Isolation can tune it toward believing that we need nobody.

So we tune it again.

And again.

And again.

For me, the process has gradually become simple. When life becomes uncertain, return to God. When work becomes disappointing, return to perspective. When relationships become difficult, return to the roots before cutting the branch.

The situation may remain exactly the same.

But the mind facing the situation will not.

Conclusion

Perhaps maturity is not reaching a point where difficult thoughts stop appearing. Perhaps maturity is learning what to do when they arrive.

I cannot control every closed door. I cannot control every decision another person makes about me. I cannot control every disagreement inside a family. I cannot understand everything God allows to happen.

But I can continue working on the mind that meets those situations.

Faith can keep hardship from turning into hopelessness. Perspective can keep disappointment from turning into bitterness. Family can remind us that independence does not require isolation.

After roughly thirty years of life, I certainly do not claim to have understood everything. If anything, growing older has shown me how much remains unknown.

But one lesson continues becoming clearer:

A peaceful life is not created only by arranging the world around us.

Sometimes it begins by tuning the world within us.

*"You may not always be able to tune the circumstances around you, but you can keep tuning the mind that has to walk through them."*
