The More I Learn, the Less Certain I Become

A reflection on how learning does not always give us more answers, sometimes it teaches us how much we still do not know.

JJ

By Jedidiah Jsk

Oct 2026 • 10 min read

The more I learn, the more I notice something unexpected. Learning gives me answers, but at the same time, it creates new questions. Something that once looked simple begins to have layers, exceptions, different perspectives, and areas I had never even considered.

When we first learn something, there can be a feeling of confidence. We understand a few concepts, connect a few points, and begin to feel that we know the subject. But when we go deeper, we start discovering how much exists beyond those first few lessons.

That has slowly changed the way I think about education. I don't believe education should only make us confident about what we know. It should also reveal the size of what we don't know.

"Sometimes we feel certain not because we know enough, but because we haven't yet discovered how much there is to know."

1. When a Little Knowledge Feels Complete

There is something interesting about being a beginner. We usually see only what has been introduced to us. If we learn ten important things about a subject and understand all ten, it can feel like we understand the subject itself. What we cannot yet see are the hundred questions sitting behind those ten answers.

This happens in almost every area of life. Learn a little about technology and it may look straightforward until you discover how many different systems, languages, architectures, security concerns, and technologies exist behind what you use every day. Learn a law and you may initially think knowing the rule is enough, until you begin discovering interpretation, evidence, procedure, precedent, exceptions, and the circumstances of each case.

I think the same thing happens outside formal education. We may hear one side of someone's story and become certain about who was right or wrong. We may experience something once and believe we understand how it always works. Sometimes certainty arrives very quickly because we have seen only a small part of the picture.

"A little knowledge can make the world look simple. Deeper knowledge shows us how much of the picture was still outside the frame."

2. Every Answer Reveals Another Question

One of the things I find most interesting about learning is that an answer does not always close a question. Sometimes an answer opens five more. You understand what something does, then you want to know how it works. You understand how it works, then you want to know why it works that way. Eventually, one question becomes an entire path of learning.

This is why I don't think saying "I don't know" should always be seen as weakness. Sometimes it is the most accurate answer an educated person can give. There is a difference between not knowing because we never tried to learn and recognizing that our current knowledge is not enough to make a confident conclusion.

The more we learn, the more important that difference becomes. We can still make decisions, form opinions, and trust what we have learned. But we can also leave room for another fact, another perspective, or better evidence to change what we currently understand.

"Knowledge does not only give us answers, sometimes its greatest gift is showing us which questions we never knew to ask."

3. Education Should Create Humility, Not Just Confidence

Education should make us more capable. A degree, a book, professional experience, research, or years of practice should give us knowledge that we did not have before. But I think education becomes more meaningful when confidence grows together with humility.

A person can be highly educated in one field and know very little about another. A doctor may understand the human body in ways an engineer does not. An engineer may understand machines and systems in ways a lawyer does not. A lawyer may understand law in ways neither of them does. Even within the same profession, one person's knowledge and experience may be completely different from another's.

In my own life, by around thirty, I have two diplomas, two degrees, an MBA, and more than ten technical and coding courses. I mention this not because these qualifications make me certain about more things, but because they have taught me almost the opposite. Every subject I studied opened another door, and behind that door I usually found many more things I had never known existed.

If anything, education has made me more comfortable saying, "I don't know." What I have learned is meaningful to me, but it has also taught me not to enter every room believing I am the smartest person in it. Almost every person, profession, subject, and experience may contain something I have not yet learned.

"The purpose of education is not only to show us how much we know, but to reveal the size of what we still don't."

The Expanding Circle

I sometimes imagine knowledge as a circle. Everything inside represents what I currently know, while everything outside represents what I do not know yet. As I read, study, work, ask questions, make mistakes, meet different people, and experience more of life, that circle grows.

But something interesting happens as it becomes larger: its edge becomes larger too. The more my knowledge grows, the more places I discover where what I know meets something I still don't understand.

The circle becomes bigger, but so does my awareness of what lies beyond it. Education has not made me know less. It has simply allowed me to see more clearly how large the unknown really is.

"The more my circle of knowledge grows, the larger the edge becomes where what I know meets what I don't."

Conclusion

There was a time when I thought learning was mainly about collecting answers. Today, I see it differently. Learning has also taught me to listen longer, question my own conclusions, and become more comfortable saying, "I don't know."

That does not mean losing confidence in what we have learned. It means holding that confidence with enough humility to accept that another fact, another experience, or another person may teach us something we have not yet considered.

Maybe that is one of the greatest things education can give us: not the feeling that we have finally understood everything, but the curiosity to keep learning after realizing that we never will.

"The more I learn, the less certain I become - not because I know less, but because I can finally see how much more there is to know."
