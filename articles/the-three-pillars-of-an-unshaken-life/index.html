The Three Pillars of an Unshaken Life

Three lessons I discovered in someone who never knew she was teaching me.

JJ

By Jedidiah Jsk

Jul 2026 • 8 min read

There are teachers who enter our lives carrying books. There are teachers who stand before classrooms. And then there are teachers we may never notice.

Their lessons do not always come through advice.

Sometimes, a person teaches us without ever trying to teach us.

I met such a person the greatest - someone the world may never know as a teacher, but whom I have come to regard as one of the greatest teachers of my life.

She may never know how carefully I observed certain parts of her character, or how deeply they stayed with me. The lessons were not spoken to me as principles. There was no lecture, no explanation and perhaps not even an intention to teach.

I simply saw them in the way she lived.

And the more I looked beyond the surface and tried to understand the heart behind her behaviour, the more I began to recognize three unusual qualities.

Over time, I did not merely admire them. I learned them. I began questioning my own behaviour through them, and eventually I tried to make them part of my own life.

That is why, from my perspective, I think of her as one of the greatest unknown teachers in the world.

Unknown, because the world may never know what she taught.

A teacher, because I do.

Some of the greatest teachers never know that they have taught us anything.

The first thing I noticed was her relationship with what she did not have.

She did not seem to run endlessly after what was absent from her life.

This does not mean that wanting something is wrong. Most of us have needs, ambitions and dreams. We naturally work for things we do not yet have. That is part of being human.

What impressed me was the way she accepted the things she did not have. She did not endlessly chase after them, nor did she allow their absence to disturb the peace she already had.

The second thing was even more beautiful.

Whatever she had, she did not appear to carry it with pride.

There was a quietness in the way she held the good things in her life, almost as though possession did not automatically mean ownership forever.

It reminded me of the spirit found in the biblical story of Job: what is given can be received with gratitude, and if life takes it away, our entire identity need not disappear with it.

What we have can be appreciated without becoming a reason to place ourselves above something.

And then there was the third lesson.

When something was lost, she did not seem to build a permanent home inside that loss.

She found a way to move her mind toward what remained.

Instead of allowing what had gone to consume everything still present, she turned toward the life that was still in her hands.

That does not mean loss never hurts.

It means loss does not have to receive the rest of our life as well.

Perhaps strength is not feeling no pain. Perhaps it is knowing where to place the mind after pain arrives.

These three qualities stayed with me:

Not being ruled by what you do not have.

Not becoming proud because of what you have.

Not remaining imprisoned by what you lost.

I first saw them in her.

Then I understood them.

Then I learned from them.

And finally, I began trying to add them to my own life. (We become better by borrowing the best from those we meet.)

The rest of this article is my attempt to pass these lessons forward to show why these three simple qualities can become three extraordinary pillars of how a person lives.

Peace begins when possession stops deciding the condition of the heart.

The First Pillar: Do Not Become a Prisoner of What Is Missing

There will almost always be something we do not have.

Someone has a larger house.

Someone earns more.

Someone received the opportunity we wanted.

Someone found love earlier.

Someone travelled farther.

Someone appears to be living the life we once imagined for ourselves.

The human mind has a strange habit. It can stand inside a beautiful garden and spend all its time looking at the one flower growing beyond the wall.

Wanting something is not wrong.

Desire has built cities, created art, discovered medicines, started businesses and moved human beings toward better lives everyday.

The problem begins when desire stops being a direction and becomes a condition for happiness.

There is an enormous difference between thinking:

“I would like to have this.”

and believing:

“I cannot be happy until I have this.”

The first gives us direction.

The second gives away our freedom.

Want something deeply, but never hand it the keys to your happiness.

We often imagine peace waiting somewhere in the future.

After the promotion.

After the house.

After the recognition.

After the relationship.

After the bank balance reaches a certain number.

We keep postponing life until life satisfies our conditions.

But the horizon has an ancient trick.

Walk toward it, and it moves.

There will always be another milestone.

Another comparison.

Another desire.

Another version of “enough.”

The person who learns to remain peaceful while something is still missing discovers a rare kind of freedom.

Ambition remains.

But desperation disappears.

You can work hard without becoming restless.

You can dream without becoming miserable.

You can pursue tomorrow without insulting today.

That is the first pillar.

The Second Pillar: Do Not Build a Throne From What You Have

If absence creates desire, possession creates another danger.

Pride.

There is nothing wrong with enjoying success.

There is nothing wrong with celebrating achievement.

But somewhere between gratitude and superiority, the human mind can quietly lose its balance.

Money becomes status.

Knowledge becomes arrogance.

Beauty becomes vanity.

Position becomes power over others.

Achievement becomes identity.

Eventually, the things we possess begin possessing us.

Gratitude says, “I am fortunate to have this.” Pride says, “I am greater because I have this.”

The difference may appear small.

It is enormous.

Think of a tree filled with fruit.

The more fruit its branches carry, the lower they bend.

Human beings often do the opposite.

The more we acquire, the higher we try to stand.

Perhaps wisdom asks us to become more like the tree.

Let achievement make us useful, not arrogant.

Let knowledge make us curious, not dismissive.

Let wealth increase generosity, not distance.

Let authority increase responsibility, not ego.

Everything we possess carries an invisible temporary label.

Our titles can change.

Our bodies age.

Markets fall.

Companies disappear.

Applause moves toward someone else.

Even extraordinary talent eventually meets time.

So perhaps the healthiest relationship with success is not absolute ownership, but stewardship.

For some period of life, something has been placed in our hands.

Use it well.

Enjoy it.

Share it.

Protect it.

But never confuse what you possess with who you are.

Hold your achievements in your hands, never above your head.

That is the second pillar.

The Third Pillar: Do Not Spend the Present Living Inside the Past

The first pillar concerns what never arrived.

The second concerns what is here.

The third may be the hardest.

It concerns what left.

A person.

A friendship.

A job.

A home.

An opportunity.

Money.

Youth.

Status.

A dream.

Sometimes even an earlier version of ourselves.

Loss is unavoidable because change is unavoidable.

Yet the mind has a remarkable ability to keep departed things alive.

The event ends, but we replay it.

The person leaves, but the conversations continue inside us.

The opportunity disappears, but we construct imaginary worlds around what might have happened.

The calendar moves forward while part of us remains standing at an old door.

Memory is a place to visit, not a house in which to live.

This does not mean we should become emotionless.

Grief deserves respect.

Some losses change us permanently.

Acceptance does not mean pretending something never mattered.

In fact, grief often exists precisely because something mattered enormously.

But there comes a point when remembering something and continuing to suffer because of it become two different things.

We can honour what was without demanding that it return.

A river does not negotiate with the water that has already passed.

It receives what comes next.

Perhaps we must learn the same wisdom.

What left us may have given us love.

It may have given us pain.

It may have given us knowledge.

Sometimes it gave us all three.

Take the lesson.

Carry the gratitude.

Leave the chains.

That is the third pillar.

Three Pillars, Three Relationships With Time

At first, these three lessons appear separate.

They are not.

They are three relationships with time.

What we do not have usually belongs to an imagined future.

What we have belongs to the present.

What we lost belongs to the past.

And human beings can become trapped in all three.

We chase the future.

We glorify the present.

We mourn the past.

Meanwhile, life quietly continues.

The future asks for effort. The present asks for humility. The past asks for acceptance.

This may be the deeper architecture behind the three pillars.

Toward what has not arrived: patience.

Toward what has arrived: humility.

Toward what has departed: acceptance.

Together, they create balance.

The Open Hand

Imagine holding something valuable in your hand.

If your hand is empty, you do not have to hate the emptiness.

If something beautiful is placed there, you do not have to believe it makes you greater than the person beside you.

And if one day it leaves your hand, you do not have to spend the rest of your life clutching the memory of it.

Perhaps life asks us to hold things with an open hand.

Our dreams.

Our achievements.

Our relationships.

Our possessions.

Even our identities.

The problem begins when the open hand becomes a closed fist.

We grip desire.

We grip success.

We grip memory.

Then we wonder why living hurts.

An open hand can receive.

An open hand can give.

And when the time comes, an open hand can let go.

Wisdom is not having nothing. Wisdom is understanding that nothing has to remain forever for life to remain meaningful.

This Is Not a Philosophy Against Ambition

These principles can easily be misunderstood.

They do not ask us to stop wanting a better life.

They do not ask us to reject success.

They do not ask us to become indifferent when something meaningful disappears.

They ask for something more difficult.

Freedom within ambition.

A person who does not collapse over what is missing can take greater risks.

A person who is not intoxicated by success can make clearer decisions.

A person who can accept loss can begin again.

That is strength.

Build.

Compete.

Create.

Earn.

Love.

Dream enormously.

But keep your inner life independent of the scoreboard.

When you fail, learn.

When you succeed, remain grounded.

When something ends, honour the chapter and turn the page.

The purpose is not detachment from life.

It is freedom within life.

The Quiet Test

Perhaps these three pillars can become a simple test we occasionally give ourselves.

Think about something you deeply want but do not yet have.

Can you pursue it without allowing its absence to poison today?

Now think about something valuable you currently possess.

Can you enjoy it without believing that having it makes you more important than someone who does not?

Finally, remember something meaningful that life has taken away.

Can you honour what it meant without allowing its absence to consume what remains?

These are uncomfortable questions.

That is why they matter.

Character is rarely revealed by what we say when everything is comfortable.

It appears in our relationship with wanting, having and losing.

A peaceful person is not someone life has spared. It is someone life has failed to enslave.

Conclusion

Life is continuously moving things through three invisible doors.

Through one door are the things we hope will enter our lives.

Through another are the things temporarily standing beside us.

Through the last are the things whose time with us has ended.

We cannot control all three doors.

But we can decide who we become while standing between them.

A person tortured by what is missing will always feel poor, regardless of how much they eventually acquire.

A person made arrogant by what is present will eventually discover how temporary possession can be.

A person imprisoned by what is gone may become so busy looking backward that they fail to see what is arriving.

There is another way.

Seek without desperation.

Possess without arrogance.

Lose without losing yourself.

Perhaps this is what the unknown teacher was pointing toward all along.

Not a life without ambition.

Not a life without achievement.

Not even a life without sorrow.

Something far more difficult:

A life in which none of them becomes your master.

Maybe that is one of the quietest definitions of freedom.

Do not measure your life only by what entered your hands. Measure it also by what never gained control of your heart.

Three pillars.

Do not be ruled by what is missing.

Do not become proud because of what is present.

Do not surrender your remaining life to what has passed.

Everything else will continue to change.

But if these three pillars remain standing, perhaps we can remain standing too.
